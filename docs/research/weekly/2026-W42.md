* **Concept:** Sequential conformal prediction for time series, with a focus on the Autocorrelated multistep conformal prediction (AcMCP) method for generating prediction intervals.
* **Market analogue:** The univariate time series $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^T$ corresponds to the daily close price of a CFD instrument (e.g., USDSGD). The forecast horizon $h$ is 1 day. The forecast error $e_{t+h|t}$ is the difference between the actual future daily close price and its point forecast. The nonconformity score $s_{t+h|t}$ is derived from this forecast error (e.g., absolute or signed error). The conformal threshold $q_{t,h}$ determines the width of the prediction interval $\widehat{C}^{\alpha}_{t+h|t}$ for the future market price. The 'interval width' itself serves as an analogue for market predictability or uncertainty at a given horizon. The analogy predicts that reliable uncertainty quantification can improve trading decisions. This could be wrong if the properties observed in electricity markets (e.g., AcMCP producing the narrowest intervals) do not transfer to highly non-stationary financial markets like FX or crypto, or if "No method is best on every criterion, and these rankings are specific to the data and forecasting setup." Additionally, "Neither property [marginal validity nor long-run validity] implies conditional coverage at each origin, coverage in every local window, or simultaneous coverage of the forecast path," whereas trading decisions are inherently conditional and local. Finally, even if intervals are reliably narrow, they might still be too wide to provide actionable signals that overcome trading costs.

* **Hypothesis:** For the USDSGD CFD, a 20-day trend-following strategy will achieve a higher Sharpe ratio after costs if trade initiation is conditional on the `AcMCP` 90% prediction interval for the next day's close price being narrower than its 20-day historical median width, compared to initiating trades on all trend-following signals.

The null hypothesis is that the Sharpe ratio of the filtered strategy is not statistically significantly higher than that of the baseline strategy, or that the filtered strategy's Sharpe ratio after costs is not positive. Failure occurs if the filtered strategy's Sharpe ratio is not higher than the baseline's, or if the difference is not significant (e.g., p-value > 0.01), or if the filtered strategy's Sharpe ratio after costs is not positive.
